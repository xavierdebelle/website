Colour basics, in plain English

The wheel

Every colour lives somewhere on the circle. Go around it and the colour itself changes — red, yellow, green, blue, back to red. Go towards the middle and it gets paler and greyer; towards the edge, stronger. The Brightness slider makes it lighter or darker.

The handles

Each dot on the wheel is one colour in your palette. The dot with a ring is your main colour; the others are worked out from it. Drag any dot and the rest follow. Choose Custom to move them one at a time.

Harmonies, one line each

  • Analogous — next-door neighbours. Calm.
  • Monochromatic — one colour, lighter and darker. Safe.
  • Complementary — opposite sides. Maximum pop.
  • Split complementary — pop, but gentler.
  • Triad / Square — evenly spaced. Lively, busy.
  • Shades — one colour, dark to light.

Don't use them equally

The 60-30-10 rule: one colour covers about 60% of the design, a second about 30%, and a bright accent the last 10%. Five colours in equal amounts almost never works.

Can people read text on it?

That's what the Contrast grid answers. AAA is excellent, AA is fine for normal text, AA large is only good for big headings, and Fail means don't put text there.

Colour blindness

About one man in twelve can't tell some reds and greens apart. Use the Normal vision menu to see your palette their way. If two colours turn into the same colour, don't rely on them alone to mean different things.

Hex codes

A code like #FFE05C is the colour's exact address. Paste it into Photoshop, Lightroom, Canva or a website and you get the same colour. Click any code here to copy it.

Screens vs. paint

This is the screen's wheel: it mixes light, so yellow sits opposite blue. A painter's wheel mixes pigment and puts yellow opposite purple. For anything seen on a screen or printed from a photo file, this is the one to trust.
